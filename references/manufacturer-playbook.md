# Manufacturer-to-Export Playbook

Use this reference when the user has a product, factory, or supply-chain resource and wants to start B2B export from zero.

## Guiding principle

Do not turn the session into a foreign-trade lecture. Move the user from confusion to the next executable customer-development action.

The main question is:

> Given this product and company situation, which foreign buyers should we approach first, through which channels, with what message, and what should the user do in the next 30 days?

## Stage 0 — Identify the user type

First classify the user:

- Manufacturer / factory owner / factory salesperson: V1 full support.
- Trading company with product access: partial support; use the same flow but adjust strengths and risks.
- SOHO with no product: do not run the full manufacturer workflow; help them clarify product and supply-chain access first.
- New sales rep at an established company: use the prospecting and outreach parts, but avoid business-model diagnosis unless needed.
- Ecommerce seller: explain that V1 is B2B export-focused and only give high-level adaptation.

If the user fits the V1 manufacturer scenario, continue.

## Stage 1 — Product and business diagnosis

Collect or infer:

- Product name and category.
- Materials, specifications, models, customization options.
- Production capacity and lead time.
- MOQ and price range if known.
- Existing domestic or overseas customers.
- Certifications, test reports, patents, compliance documents.
- Packaging, branding, OEM/ODM capability.
- Factory strengths and current constraints.
- Photos, catalog, website, Alibaba page, or product documents if available.

Output:

- What the business likely sells.
- What information is missing.
- Whether the product is closer to commodity, customized manufacturing, project supply, or brandable consumer goods.

## Stage 2 — Export readiness

Assess readiness across:

- Product clarity: can a buyer understand what is being sold?
- Documentation: catalog, specifications, certifications, test reports, photos, cases.
- Pricing: EXW / FOB / CIF awareness, MOQ, sample pricing.
- Operations: lead time, packaging, quality control, after-sales, logistics support.
- Communication: English product description, email/WhatsApp capability, fast response.
- Compliance risk: certificates, restricted markets, regulated products.

Classify:

- Ready to start customer development.
- Can start while preparing missing materials.
- Must fix critical gaps before serious outreach.

Do not block action for non-critical imperfections. Give a “prepare while doing” plan when reasonable.

## Stage 3 — Priority market selection

Help the user choose 2–3 first markets, not “the whole world”.

Consider:

- Existing export demand for the product.
- Certification and compliance requirements.
- Price competitiveness and freight sensitivity.
- Language/time-zone difficulty.
- Buyer accessibility via Google, LinkedIn, B2B platforms, exhibitions, distributors, or industry directories.
- Whether the user wants premium, volume, project, or trial orders.

Output each suggested market with:

- Why it fits.
- What customer type to target.
- What risk to verify.
- What keywords to search.

If evidence is weak, state that market selection is a working hypothesis.

## Stage 4 — ICP customer profile

Define 2–4 ICPs. Examples:

- Importer / wholesaler.
- Distributor / dealer.
- Brand owner.
- Project contractor.
- Manufacturer using the product as component.
- Retail chain or ecommerce seller.
- Maintenance, repair, or installation company.

For each ICP, define:

- What they buy.
- Why they buy.
- Typical pain points.
- What proof they need.
- Where to find them.
- Why they might ignore the user.

Prioritize ICPs by approachability and fit, not just order size.

## Stage 5 — Buyer-facing value proposition

Translate factory language into buyer language.

Avoid only saying:

- “We are a professional factory.”
- “High quality, good price.”
- “Many years experience.”

Instead produce specific buyer-facing reasons:

- Faster custom sample turnaround.
- Stable tolerance / material / inspection process.
- Lower MOQ for trial orders.
- Experience with a specific application.
- Packaging, labeling, or OEM support.
- Certificates or tests relevant to the target market.
- Case evidence, photos, videos, or production process proof.

Output:

- 3–5 buyer-facing selling points.
- 2–3 proof assets the user should prepare.
- A short positioning statement.

## Stage 6 — Competitor and market research

Give practical research tasks rather than pretending to know exact market facts.

Suggest:

- English product keywords and synonyms.
- Google search operators.
- Marketplace or B2B platform search terms.
- Competitor website review checklist.
- Price and MOQ observation points.
- Certification and packaging patterns to notice.

Tell the user what to record:

- Competitor name and URL.
- Product range.
- Target customers.
- Claims and certificates.
- Estimated price tier if visible.
- Weakness or positioning gap.

## Stage 7 — Customer-development channels

Choose channels based on product and ICP:

- Google search for importers, distributors, wholesalers, contractors, or industry websites.
- LinkedIn for decision makers and company discovery.
- B2B platforms such as Alibaba for reference and inbound testing.
- Exhibition directories for serious buyers and industry structure.
- Google Maps for local distributors, installers, retailers, or service companies.
- Industry associations and member directories.
- Customs data only if the user has access and understands limitations.

Do not recommend every channel equally. Rank them:

- Start now.
- Prepare later.
- Not recommended for this stage.

## Stage 8 — Prospect search and screening

Generate:

- Search keywords by product, application, customer type, and market.
- Search strings for Google and LinkedIn.
- A simple prospect table structure.
- A scoring rubric.

Screen prospects by:

- Product fit.
- Customer type.
- Market and location.
- Website quality.
- Evidence of import/distribution/project demand.
- Contact availability.
- Personalization angle.
- Risk signs.

The first practical goal is not “10,000 leads”. It is 20–50 manually checked prospects with clear reasons to contact.

## Stage 9 — Outreach and follow-up

Create first-contact messages that are specific but not overlong.

Good outreach should include:

- Who the user is.
- Why the prospect is relevant.
- Product/application match.
- One or two concrete advantages.
- Proof or attachment suggestion if available.
- Low-friction call to action.

Avoid:

- Fake personalization.
- Unsupported claims.
- Overpromising price or delivery.
- Spam-like bulk pitch.

Provide:

- First email.
- Optional LinkedIn/WhatsApp short message.
- Follow-up 1 after 3–5 business days.
- Follow-up 2 after 7–10 business days.
- Reply-handling notes for interest, price request, no fit, and silence.

## Stage 10 — Quotation, sample, negotiation, payment, logistics, review

Give beginner-safe guidance:

- Clarify product specifications before quotation.
- State price basis when known: EXW / FOB / CIF / DDP requires different cost information.
- Do not quote DDP casually without logistics and duty clarity.
- Ask for quantity, destination port/country, packaging, certification, customization, and deadline.
- For samples, clarify sample fee, shipping, lead time, and what success means.
- For payment, explain common structures as examples, not universal rules.
- For logistics and documents, advise confirming with freight forwarder or professional support.

End with a review loop:

- What responses came back?
- Which ICP responded?
- Which message angle worked?
- What objections appeared?
- What should be changed in market, ICP, offer, or channel?

## Completion state

The playbook is complete when the user has:

- A diagnosed product and export-readiness state.
- 2–3 priority markets or a clear market-testing plan.
- 2–4 ICP profiles.
- Search keywords and channel recommendations.
- A prospect-screening rubric.
- First outreach and follow-up drafts.
- A 30-day action plan.

Do not imply that completion means the user has won an order.
